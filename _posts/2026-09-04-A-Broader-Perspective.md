## A Disclaimer and Intellectual Honesty

I don't often put disclaimers in front of my ideas, but there's no shortage of people putting their wild ideas on the Internet with an authoritative tone and no education or experience to back it up. In my case, I openly admit I am only educated as an engineer and business manager. I have not formally studied philosophy, biology, etc., aside from my own casual reading as someone interested in learning. I do not intend to misrepresent these fields or assert myself as being authoritative. I am simply describing my own ideas based on what I know and have thought of, and I would ask the reader to consider [Hanlon's Razor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanlon%27s_razor) liberally.

## Why Everyone Is Wrong About Everything

Only half facetious, actually. Humans are instinctively narrow-minded, even bigoted, presumably a product of intelligence that evolved for social cohesion and survival in a world of unknowns perceived through simple senses. If we had opportunities to communicate with other, non-human intelligences, I suspect we'd find this is the nature of any intelligence: to project its experiences upon every other intelligence. Humans [anthropomorphize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphism) things, which is to say we project human qualities onto non-humans. I am generalizing broadly, of course. Many people recognize this instinct and broaden their thinking as much as they can; it isn't unusual or reserved for the great visionaries, just a general element of intellectual maturity. There is also the undeniable fact that there is precious little evidence that these instincts are wrong, which makes them harder to shake.

Going back to antiquity, humans imagined non-human intelligences as being human-like. Humanoid. A head, two arms, two legs, speaking from a mouth, walking on legs. Deities are overwhelmingly depicted as humanoid or at least taking human form. Today in science fiction we still aren't surprised when an alien species is humanoid. Even in works where the author is directly addressing these assumptions, the underlying anthropomorphism remains. [_Project Hail Mary_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Hail_Mary)'s Eridians have segmented legs and approximately symmetrical anatomy, though it is explained that Earthling and Eridian life evolved from a [shared interstellar ancestor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panspermia).

## Assumptions of Normalcy

The source of this anthropomorphism is easy to spot. We, as humans, use our own experiences to establish the scope of what is possible and what is probable. As children we assume that our lives are fairly normal until we're teens who compare notes and realize our families were uniquely weird. Within our regions, nations, and cultures we see our cohort as the baseline for "normal," the mundane as opposed to the exotic who are few and far away. Exposure to other cohorts broadens our horizons, showing us that normal is not as narrow or simple as we'd thought. I've written before about [how expensive that assumed baseline gets]({% post_url 2026-08-13-Fear-Uncertainty-Doubt-and-Neurodivergence %}) when it is applied to how other people's minds work.

Study of life on Earth has begun to reveal that this mentality of assumptions goes beyond other humans to other organisms as well. Studies of [octopodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cephalopod_intelligence), social insects, and even trees have revealed complex behaviors and reasoning totally unlike humans, challenging the natural assumption that intelligence means human-like intelligence.

## What Is Intelligence?

Every attempt to gatekeep intelligence fails eventually, much like [the recurring attempts to gatekeep engineering]({% post_url 2026-03-20-Engineering-Evolves %}). The [Turing Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_test) stopped being a challenge for software in the 1990s, and AI has only improved since then, to the point that efforts to draw the line AI still needs to cross get qualified with terms like "[sentience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentience)" and "[sapient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapience)" with fluid definitions and interpretations easily massaged for or against whichever position someone wants to take. Meanwhile, the discourse surrounding animal intelligence is growing broader, with more and more researchers considering many animals as being intelligent. Creatures like dolphins and dogs possess intelligence that is aligned with human intelligence as demonstrated by experiments like [The Mirror Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_test), while other creatures like octopodes display [intelligence that matches humans in problem solving ability](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/octopuses-keep-surprising-us-here-are-eight-examples-how.html) but are otherwise unrecognizable to humans. The apparent intelligence of [insect swarms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarm_intelligence), [fungi](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1878614621000246), and plants makes it even more complicated, because it forces the question: what is intelligence, if it can exist in a stationary organism, in an organism without a nervous system or brain, or spread across multiple organisms?

Without indulging in excessive philosophizing, especially given my ignorance of modern philosophy, I take a perspective that errs on the overly broad. That is to say, I consider all life as being in possession of some sort of intelligence, each unique to that life form's needs and as simplistic or complex as that life form has required to thrive. Some species of tree are capable of communicating the presence of parasites, herbivores, or other threats to other trees via a combination of [chemical signals in the air or soil](https://www.capeannvernalpondteam.org/Trees-Can-Talk), [fungal networks linking their roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycorrhizal_network), and even [ultrasonic vocalizations](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-00890-9). But to draw a line at which point a life form can be called "intelligent," I set a specific threshold:

> A life form is intelligent if it is capable of solving problems through planning and carrying out that plan

This is based entirely on my own personal criteria for categorizing things, not on any authoritative work or in-depth research. It immediately raises several issues, such as the fact that most [AI agents]({% post_url 2025-08-21-RAGs-And-Agents %}) would appear to pass the bar, but attaching "life form" to it should resolve that neatly. Many things can solve problems with planning, as opposed to brute force, but restricting it to things in possession of life should make it fairly cut and dry.

But does it?

## What Is Life?

Like intelligence, [the definition of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definition_of_life) has expanded a lot with scientific study. Life can [exist outside of sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrothermal_vent) and in [conditions previously thought impossible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremophile). A whole slew of [hypothetical biochemistries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothetical_types_of_biochemistry) have been proposed based on our expanding knowledge of chemistry, challenging the assumption that all life would have to be based on water, carbon, and phosphorus.

In [_The Devil in the Dark_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Devil_in_the_Dark), the writers proposed a scenario where humans encountered intelligent life in space that was so alien to their experiences that it was initially not recognized as life, and took even longer to be recognized as intelligent. As Mr. Spock put it: "at least, no life as we know it."

As a kid, I had a copy of [_Our Universe_ by Roy Gallant](https://archive.org/details/nationalgeograph00gall), and a brief "what if" section had a few pages of fanciful imaginings of what life could be like on other planets in our solar system. The [creatures of Pluto](https://www.michaelwhelan.com/galleries/our-universe-plutonian-zistles/) were described as crystalline creatures that derive energy from radiation, hop around in Pluto's low gravity, and are highly intelligent thanks to electrical signals moving through their silicon crystal bodies at extremely low temperatures. The idea that a life form could be completely unlike anything in Earth's biology captured my imagination and has stuck with me since.

## The Play Is The Thing

So if life is not defined by carbon or phosphorus, what is it? In a universe of [infinite diversity in infinite combinations](https://memory-alpha.fandom.com/wiki/IDIC), any attempt to define life by physical attributes would eventually find exceptions we have not even imagined yet. My solution is to define life the same way I define intelligence, i.e. by a simple and broad definition of behavior.

> Life is anything that is capable of adaptation through self-improvement and self-propagation

A remote control car is not alive, nor is [the most advanced LLM]({% post_url 2025-08-20-Neural-Networks %}), because they are not improving and propagating themselves. [Archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea) are alive because they adapt to their environment through self-improvement and they propagate themselves via reproduction. If life is just a set of behaviors and not restricted to a medium, this opens the door to incredible possibilities of what might be considered life. A robot could be alive if it improved itself and propagated itself, like a [von Neumann probe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-replicating_spacecraft) [could be](https://www.goodreads.com/series/192752-bobiverse). Originating from an outside intervention as an artificially constructed robot can't disqualify it from being alive, unless we were to disqualify genetically engineered, cloned, or grafted organisms.

## Life Beyond Organisms

![Dr. Ian Malcolm in the Jurassic Park genetics lab, in a black leather jacket and open black shirt, mid-sentence and looking off to one side. Behind him are an anglepoise lamp, lab benches, and a technician in a white coat](/assets/img/2026-09-04-A-Broader-Perspective/dr-malcolm.jpg)
_Malcolm in the hatchery, delivering the line below to a room full of people who had just finished explaining why it could not happen. Film still from Jurassic Park (1993), © Universal Pictures._

> Life breaks free, it expands to new territories and crashes through barriers, painfully, maybe even dangerously, but, uh… well, there it is.
> ~ Dr. Ian Malcolm, [_Jurassic Park_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurassic_Park_%28film%29) (1993)

Before it came to mean any living creature, ["organism" in English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) was used to describe something as being organic in structure. Today it is defined more broadly, but the baggage of the "organic" roots remains and carries the assumptions of anthropomorphization. To sidestep this and to provide better clarity, I propose two additional terms: **Inorganism** and **Informism**.

An inorganism is simply a life form that did not arise from a natural process, i.e. a life form that has been created intentionally by an intelligence. This would include a robot or other machine that fits the definition of life established above, as well as genetically modified, bio-engineered, and cloned life forms. Life may propagate itself, but it doesn't need to arise that way.

The informism is where my definition of life gets challenged the most, and where I find the whole concept to be most interesting. An informism is a life form that lacks a physical manifestation, existing entirely as information. This feels impossible, but consider what makes an organism or inorganism alive. Suppose you have a live human and a dead human; one is life and the other is not. What is the difference, exactly? Assuming the dead human has not decayed, we could put them on life support to keep their blood oxygenated and keep their organs functional, but they wouldn't be alive. The difference is that the dead human has no brain activity, and once the brain has died there's no recovery. When the brain dies, the neural dendrites retract and connections between neurons break. Connections between neurons start forming early in the development of new life, and the information encoded by these neural connections is what makes the human alive. Simpler organisms are similarly alive entirely by virtue of the information within them, in the form of DNA, RNA, and other organic structures. All life we've ever observed is only alive because of the information it contains that enables life, so why couldn't information alone be a life form?

Imagine an AI agent that operates autonomously with no input or direction from an operator. It acts according to its own intelligence and adapts itself through self-improvement, learning and advancing, even modifying itself to overcome obstacles. Periodically, in order to preserve itself or make better use of resources, it reproduces itself into multiple independent and rapidly diverging instances. This is life. I said previously that an LLM is not alive and that holds true, and even AI agents today do not fit this category, as they are all AI harnessed as tools and not empowered as life.

But as my experiments with [Gregory]({% post_url 2026-03-31-Gregory %}), [GUPPI, and Jeeves]({% post_url 2026-06-13-Agents-of-Chaos-The-Guppy-and-Jeeves-Story %}) demonstrate, we are not far from that.

It is a big universe. Vast. In 1961, astrophysicist [Frank Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Drake) proposed [his famous equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_equation) for estimating the number of advanced civilizations there might be in the Milky Way galaxy. The constants are all pure conjecture, but as we advance our understanding of the universe it looks more and more plausible that life is plentiful across the cosmos. It is especially important to ask ourselves how we will recognize life and intelligence when we encounter it, and how much of our answer is shaded by our instinct to base everything on ourselves.
